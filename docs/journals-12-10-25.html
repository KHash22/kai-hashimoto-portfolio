RECONSTITUTION

12-10-25

In the past two months I've been working on what many would call 'self-care'. I seldom have participated in such routines in my time as a university student up to this point. I thought it made me appear weak, self-indulgent, and self-absorbed. In many ways, I wish we had better words to describe this act. The very word 'self' 'care' carries a contradictory, yet individualistic implication. 'Care' is something we provide to the weak, less fortunate, or despondent: A monk serving his community, parents tending to their baby, a PSW in a retirement home. Yet often self-care is presented in materialistic ideals. Expensive soaps and scents, a cottage vacation, overpriced coffees, kicking your feet up on an Ottoman. It would only be natural to assume those who are fit and have the resources to take such luxuries need not 'care'. This isn't caring; it's pampering.

It feels like a retreat from responsibility. It feels like letting my guard down. I should (and probably do) have better things to engage with. Study for an extra hour. Work on my app. Search for work. Activities that progress my career, so that one day, my partner, children, and community would be able to enjoy the fruits of life because of the burdens I took on. Without diverting the topic, the masculine tropes to die the heroic death I think resonates with many on this side of the chromosome pairing. In my mind, self-care appears as an antithesis to self-sacrifice.

For context, I'm a workaholic. I dream of working until the day I die. I would often be told “you're too hard on yourself”, or “maybe you should ease off a bit”. To me these statements were compliments. Insane, I know. But it validated the sense that I was doing the right thing regardless of the consequences it had on my personhood. It felt like a badge of honour to have no time but to work.

Around September I picked up the habit of washing my face after watching a Korean skincare YouTube video. My skin was poorly taken care of during the two years I was working on a major project, so the timing felt right to try something new. The video's call-to-action worked. Immediately after watching the video, I picked up the following products:

  • A generic soap-based face wash
  • Toner by Skin 1004
  • Barrier Cream by Dr. Althea

Ironically, what drew me most was the presentation. The author explained it a way that suggested the routine took very little time out of my day (~ 5 minutes), cost little to start (the above list I purchased for ~40CAD total on sale, and I expect all of it to last for another 3-4 months), and results were effective. It worked. After a week my face felt cleaner, I was complimented more, and my own self-esteem grew.

I realized what 'self-care' had done for me. It raised my standards. It introduced a new baseline of duty to be presentable in front of my peers, and a routine to reset the challenging thoughts. Even on the worst of days, so long as I washed my face, I could wash away any misgivings the next morning. By being presentable, self-assured and steadfast I could at minimum offer some comfort to those around me: a thin warm smile, a helping voice, a sociable stranger. I could take better care of my community.

Of course, one could easily make an argument that these aids could come from anyone regardless of if they participate in 'self-care'. This is true. My point is that building routines that reconstitute our fortitude, self-image, and appearance is necessary if we want to continue to work our bodies to achieve our goals and be the executor of our beliefs. Without this, we risk poor cognitive and physical hygiene. We think of delusions like “I'm not likeable”. This is only reinforced further when our physical appearance degrades. Peers worry about us. Strangers look away. I cannot control how people react to appearances, but I can at least try to raise my own standards.

I'm still a workaholic and have little plans to change that. I'm in my twenties: I only have this decade before my body begins its permanent decline. I want to achieve so many things before this opportunity closes. Even if I couldn't care less about my family history of -REDACTED-, I inevitably need to be sane, sanitary, and fit to converge on thought-out decisions and technical solutions. This is only possible by upkeeping a routine of exercise, hygiene, and timely rest.

Whatever the routine may be, I've come to observe that I have to do it without guilt or regret AND raise my own self-perception by the end of it. I can doom-scroll for three hours (I am guilty of this too) as a 'self-care' routine, but could I do it without the guilt of wasting time, and does it make me feel smarter for it? These routines also create a minimum time block that forces me to budget my time more consciously.

---

Our presentation of 'self-care' is utterly terrible, and I hope one day there are better terms to describe it. If I were to throw my hat into the ring, I'd call it reconstitution:

“I usually reconstitute in the morning by washing my face. Makes me feel sane.”

“Cleaning the house reconstitutes both myself and my living space.”

Subjectively, that sounds more self-assured than:

“My morning self-care routine is skincare. Makes me feel sane.”

“Cleaning the house self-care for both me and my living space.”

I accept the argument that this piece is exceptionally male-centric. I needed to present it in a way that is palatable to a younger me who's already adverse to 'self-care'. Drumming the same beat of this word only pushed me away, and I imagine it would be no different for many others like me. We need more people with healthier minds and bodies to solve problems larger than themselves.
