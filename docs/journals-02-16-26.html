ON BEING GROUNDED, DISCIPLINED, AND ASKING GOOD QUESTIONS

02-16-26

Last night I had an argument with my mother, because frankly I was being a prick.

We both wanted to fix the cracks in the drywall around the first-floor heat vent, so on Saturday evening I asked to fix it Sunday morning. I was dead set on fixing it Sunday, because my father kept complaining about the utility bill (whom he has no idea how much we pay per month).

Annoyed by him nagging about turning off the lights, I did a bit of research to find the division of power consumption in a household. ~40-50% is used by HVAC systems.

Things did not go as planned. Not only did we not have a shop vac, by the time we had all the materials, it was approaching 6PM (we typically have dinner at this time). Whether it be my father and sister taking the car to go for Judo, or getting caught up in other chores, we had the materials but not the time.

On the drive back from Home Depot to dinner, I complained to my mother: “Didn't get anything done today”.

That must have struck a nerve, because during dinner she was increasingly frustrated at how my tone was off throughout the day (which is likely, tone control is a work-in-progress for me), and that it's ridiculous to think I could get something done in a day.

She told me (translated):

“You shouldn't be spending your break like this. Go be young, I am off work starting Thursday, and I have time. A few days of a cracked heat vent we can both live with.”

---

My problem with doing projects or tasks is not a matter of motivation or drive: It is to not set clear expectations of when something should be done by or if it should be done at all. It's easy for me to say, “I want to build Rome in a day”. To be disappointed when it isn't, is utterly ridiculous - what was I expecting? To build Rome in a day?

For all my projects now, I know the features/requirements it needs. Moving forward I need to make a conscious effort to partition features, segment them into logical groupings, and most importantly - recognize the progress I made in that day.

---

The vent wasn't fixed on Sunday. But within the constraints we had, we collected all the materials needed and took off the heat vent from the wall. It got the project started, and my mother is motivated to fix it. This isn't a failure to complete the project. It is a failure on my part to recognize that we made the first step to fixing something.

I would like to get better at asking better questions. A great question for me subscribes to two or more of the following (4 being most important),

  • Makes the respondent share more than they planned
  • Cannot be answered in a single sentence
  • Specific - avoids terms like ‘things’, ‘in general’.
  • Structured to be specific to that person’s lived experiences - makes them the expert of a subject

Yet a good question should simultaneously be selfish. You are asking, because they’re giving out knowledge for free.

What’s a habit, observation, or life event that you’ve picked up or experienced in the last N years, that’s changed how you behave, and what experiences have come because of that?

Who would you consider a mentor, and what qualities do you see in them that you aspire for yourself?
